# Visual Ergonomics and Color Evidence

**Purpose:** record the empirical and standards evidence used for OM's default visual theme and prototype. This file distinguishes evidence-backed rules from design choices that still require OM-specific usability testing.

## 1. Display polarity and reading

Controlled proofreading studies repeatedly report a **positive-polarity advantage**: dark text on a light background produces better proofreading/legibility performance than light text on a dark background under the tested conditions. Work by Buchner, Piepenbrock, Mayr, and colleagues links this largely to luminance and pupil size, and reports the advantage as particularly relevant at small character sizes.

OM implication:

- Light mode remains the default reference palette for reading-intensive screens.
- Dark mode is a first-class peer for user preference and low-light contexts, but OM does not claim that dark mode is inherently more readable.
- Light and dark palettes are tuned independently; dark is never generated by simple inversion.

References:

- Buchner & Baumgartner, *Text-background polarity affects performance irrespective of ambient illumination and colour contrast*, Ergonomics (2007): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17510822/
- Buchner et al., *The advantage of positive text-background polarity is due to high display luminance*, Ergonomics (2009): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19562598/
- Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner, *Smaller pupil size and better proofreading performance with positive than with negative polarity displays*, Ergonomics (2014): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25135324/
- Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner, *Positive display polarity is particularly advantageous for small character sizes*, Human Factors (2014): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25141597/
- Apple HIG, Dark Mode: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/dark-mode

## 2. Contrast and redundant coding

WCAG 2.2 requires that color not be the only means of communicating essential information. It also defines contrast requirements for text, UI components, and focus indicators. Apple's accessibility guidance similarly calls for sufficient contrast in both light and dark appearances.

OM implication:

- Normal text targets >= 4.5:1 contrast; OM's default theme generally exceeds this substantially.
- UI-state boundaries that users need to identify target >= 3:1 against adjacent colors.
- Keyboard focus uses a strong visible geometry plus color, not a barely perceptible tint.
- Error, success, warning, verification, unread, selection, and presence always have a non-color cue where the distinction matters.

References:

- WCAG 2.2, Use of Color / Non-text Contrast / Focus: https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/
- W3C Understanding Non-text Contrast: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast
- W3C Understanding Focus Appearance: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-appearance
- Apple HIG, Accessibility: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility

## 3. Color can accelerate visual search

Experimental HCI work has found that color can support preattentive visual processing and improve search efficiency when applied systematically. Usability studies of visual indicators also support combining **color plus a second cue such as shape/icon**, rather than relying on either alone.

OM implication:

- Color is functional: it helps users scan for interactivity, state, and urgency before they read every label.
- OM uses a small, stable semantic color vocabulary instead of decorating unrelated elements with many hues.
- The same semantic hue must not be reused for unrelated static decoration when doing so would dilute its information scent.

References:

- B. Bednarik et al., *The role of colour preattentive processing in human-computer interaction task efficiency*, International Journal of Industrial Ergonomics 38 (2008): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169814107002211
- Nielsen Norman Group, *Visual Indicators to Differentiate Items in a List*: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-indicators-differentiators/

## 4. Familiar signifiers reduce interaction uncertainty

Blue remains a particularly strong learned cue for interactive/link-like text on the web, although modern interfaces can use other colors if signifiers remain consistent. More generally, the evidence and usability guidance favor preserving familiar visual affordances rather than stripping UI until users must guess what is actionable.

OM implication:

- The default OM accent is an accessible cobalt/azure family because it is highly familiar as an interactive hue and remains distinguishable from the conventional red/amber/green status families.
- OM does not color arbitrary static prose with the interactive accent.
- Interactive affordance also uses shape, location, iconography, hover/focus/pressed states, and labels where needed.

References:

- Nielsen Norman Group, *Beyond Blue Links: Making Clickable Elements Recognizable*: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/clickable-elements/
- Nielsen Norman Group, *Guidelines for Visualizing Links*: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/guidelines-for-visualizing-links/
- Nielsen Norman Group, *Similarity Principle in Visual Design*: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gestalt-similarity/

## 5. Color-vision deficiency is common enough to design for by default

A worldwide review reports substantial prevalence of inherited red-green color deficiency, including about 8% of men in European Caucasian populations and 4–6.5% of men in Chinese/Japanese populations in the reviewed surveys.

OM implication:

- Red versus green is never the sole distinction.
- Status roles use icon/shape/text redundancy.
- Theme QA includes color-vision-deficiency simulation and, when practical, testing with affected users.

Reference:

- Birch, *Worldwide prevalence of red-green color deficiency*, JOSA A (2012): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22472762/

## 6. Touch ergonomics supports large, reachable targets

One-handed smartphone studies show that target size and location materially affect accuracy, time, satisfaction, and muscular effort. Studies comparing 4/7/10 mm targets found 7 and 10 mm performed better on time measures, with 10 mm strongest across broader measures; other work found smaller buttons increased thumb effort/fatigue. Reach also changes with screen size, hand characteristics, and posture.

OM implication:

- 48 logical px remains the preferred compact-mobile target box; 44 is the lower normal bound unless a platform provides a larger effective hit box.
- High-frequency controls stay in reachable lower zones when practical.
- Tiny decorative icons may exist, but tiny hit targets do not.
- Reachability is configurable rather than inferred from hidden handedness profiling.

References:

- Park & Han, *Touch key design for one-handed thumb interaction with a mobile phone*, International Journal of Industrial Ergonomics (2010): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169814109001036
- Park & Han, *One-handed thumb interaction of mobile devices from the input accuracy perspective* (2010): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169814110000806
- Xiong & Muraki, *An ergonomics study of thumb movements on smartphone touch screen* (2014): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24707989/
- Trudeau et al., *Smartphone form factors: Effects of width and bottom bezel on touch performance, workload, and physical demand* (2018): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29122185/

## 7. Aesthetics matters, but does not rescue poor usability

Experimental results on the aesthetic-usability relationship are mixed in direction and strength, which is exactly why OM should not treat beauty as a substitute for usability. Some experiments found an attractive treatment improved perceived usability and task time; others found strong usability differences shaped later aesthetic judgments more than aesthetics changed usability perceptions.

OM implication:

- OM's default theme should feel intentional, pleasant, and distinctive.
- Task speed, clarity, predictability, and recovery stay higher in the priority order than decoration.
- Preset themes must all pass the same functional tests rather than being exempt because they look attractive.

References:

- Sonderegger & Sauer, *The influence of design aesthetics in usability testing*, Applied Ergonomics (2010): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003687009001148
- Tuch et al., *Is beautiful really usable?*, Computers in Human Behavior (2012): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563212000908

## 8. What the research does **not** prove

The evidence above supports the **structure** of OM's visual language: contrast, targetability, polarity options, semantic consistency, redundancy, restrained color, and familiar affordances.

It does **not** scientifically prove that one exact blue, radius, wallpaper, or theme name is universally optimal. OM's actual palette values below are engineered hypotheses that meet the evidence-backed constraints. They still require real-device and real-user evaluation.
